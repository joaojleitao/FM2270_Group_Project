1. The portfolio at a glance

What it holds: three exchange-traded funds: US large-cap equities (SPY), 7–10 year US Treasury bonds (IEF) and physical gold (GLD).
How it is managed: every month-end, each asset receives a weight inversely proportional to its recent volatility, so that the calmer assets (usually bonds) get more capital and the more volatile ones (equities, gold) get less. Holdings are rebalanced monthly.
Backtest, Jan 2006 – Oct 2026 (net of costs): 6.6% average annual return with 6.9% volatility, a Sharpe ratio of 0.71 and a worst peak-to-trough loss of 16.1%, compared with 7.8%, 9.2%, 0.66 and 29.5% for a classic 60/40 stock/bond portfolio.
In one sentence: a lower-risk alternative to a 60/40 portfolio that gave up about 1.3% of return per year in exchange for roughly a quarter less volatility and about half the maximum drawdown.

2. Target client

Who. Moderate-risk private investors in Portugal and other euro-area countries, served directly or through a wealth manager or financial adviser: for example professionals in their 40s and 50s building retirement savings, or recent retirees who keep part of their wealth invested.

Objectives and horizon. Steady growth of capital above inflation over a horizon of at least five years, with a smoother ride than an equity portfolio. The client does not need income from the portfolio and values daily liquidity.

Attitude to risk. Moderate and, above all, drawdown-averse: the client can live with ordinary fluctuations but would be tempted to sell after a loss like the 51% fall of US equities in 2008–09, or even the 29% fall of a 60/40 portfolio. Our portfolio is designed for this client: the risk level is kept roughly stable by construction, and three different return drivers are combined so that no single market dominates.

The three assets are liquid, cheap and have long histories, and their returns come from different sources: the equity risk premium, the term premium on government bonds, and gold's role as a store of value. Historically their correlations have been low or negative (daily correlation between SPY and IEF over the sample: -0.29), which is what makes a combination less risky than any one of them. They were chosen for their economic role as broad asset classes, not because of their past returns (see Section 9).

4. Allocation rule

At the last trading day of each month t, we estimate the volatility σi,t of each asset as the standard deviation of its daily returns over the previous 252 trading days (about 12 months), up to and including day t. The weights are

wi,t = (1/σi,t) / Σj (1/σj,t),   long-only, summing to 100%.

The new weights are bought at the month-end close and earn returns from the first trading day of month t+1, so every decision uses only information available at the time. Between rebalancing dates the holdings drift with prices (no daily trading). Motivation: an asset that is twice as volatile receives half the capital, so each asset contributes a similar amount of stand-alone risk. A 60/40 portfolio looks balanced in capital but is not balanced in risk: equities are about three times as volatile as 7–10 year bonds and drive most of its fluctuations. Inverse volatility is a simple, transparent step towards risk balancing that needs no return forecasts, which are notoriously unreliable. It is not full risk parity: it ignores correlations, so risk contributions are only approximately equal.

5. Data and backtest method

  • Source and adjustments: daily prices from Yahoo Finance through the yfinance API, adjusted for splits and dividends (auto_adjust=True), so returns are total returns after the ETFs' expense ratios. Risk-free rate: 3-month US Treasury bill (FRED series TB3MS, monthly, % p.a.), converted to a monthly rate ln(1 + TB3MS/100)/12; average 1.69% p.a. over the sample.
  • Sample: prices from 1 December 2004 (GLD's history starts in November 2004) to 31 October 2026, the last complete month, both fixed in the code. The first 12-month volatility estimate is available on 30 December 2005, so the evaluation period is January 2006 – October 2026 (250 months). It includes the 2008 financial crisis, the 2020 COVID crash and 2022, when stocks and bonds fell together.
  • Frequency: daily data to estimate volatility and to let holdings drift; monthly returns for all performance measures.
  • Portfolio returns: each portfolio starts in cash on 30 December 2005; asset weights are applied to simple returns (exact aggregation); performance measures use monthly log returns, as in the course. Costs: 10 basis points on every unit bought or sold, cost = 0.10% × Σ|wtarget − wdrifted|, deducted at each rebalance, including the first purchase.
  • Benchmarks (same costs and dates): the primary benchmark is 60/40 SPY/IEF rebalanced monthly, the standard moderate portfolio our client would otherwise hold. A 1/3 equal-weight mix of the same three ETFs isolates the effect of the weighting rule from the effect of adding gold. SPY alone (buy and hold) shows the equity-only alternative.

7. What the numbers mean for the client

  • Average return (6.6% p.a.). Lower than the 60/40 (7.8%) and the equal-weight mix (8.1%): $1 grew to $3.93 versus $5.11 for the 60/40. The strategy holds on average only 22% in equities, the best-performing asset of the period, so the client gives up return.
  • Volatility (6.9%). About a quarter lower than the 60/40 (9.2%) and less than half of SPY (15.1%). Risk was also more stable: the 36-month rolling volatility stayed between 4.4% and 9.9%, versus 5.1%–13.9% for the 60/40. A stable risk level is what a moderate client needs to stay invested.
  • Sharpe ratio (0.71). Excess return per unit of risk was slightly higher than the 60/40 (0.66) and SPY (0.58), but slightly lower than the naive equal-weight mix (0.74). The honest reading: most of the risk-adjusted benefit comes from adding gold to stocks and bonds; inverse-volatility weighting then trades some of that return for lower risk. The difference of 0.05 with the 60/40 is small relative to sampling noise over 21 years.
  • Beta (0.48, not annualized). When the 60/40 portfolio's excess return moves by 1%, the strategy's moves on average by about 0.48%: the client's wealth is roughly half as sensitive to the stock/bond market as a 60/40 investor's.
  • Alpha (1.92% p.a.). After adjusting for its lower beta, the strategy earned 1.92% per year more than a mix of the 60/40 and T-bills with the same beta. The (conventional OLS) t-statistic of 1.66 is below 1.96, so the alpha is not statistically significant at the 5% level, and it mostly reflects gold's strong performance, not skill.
  • Information ratio (-0.18) and tracking error (7.1%). Measured directly against the 60/40, the strategy lost 0.18 units of return per unit of deviation. A client who judges the portfolio by whether it beats a 60/40 each year will be disappointed; this is a lower-risk product, not a higher-return one.
  • Maximum drawdown (16.1%). The deepest loss from a previous peak was about half that of the 60/40 (29.5%) and a third of SPY (50.8%). In calendar year 2008 the strategy gained 2.4% while the 60/40 lost 18.0%; its worst month was 7.2% (October 2008) versus 10.3% for the 60/40. For a drawdown-averse client, this is the key result.

8. Robustness of the results

Parameters such as a 12-month window can be chosen, consciously or not, because they worked in the past. Table 2 shows that the conclusions do not depend on them: with 3- or 6-month windows, quarterly rebalancing, or trading one day later (at the close of the first trading day of each month), the Sharpe ratio stays between 0.69 and 0.75, volatility near 7% and the maximum drawdown near 16%.

9. Implementation, costs and risks

Rebalancing and costs. Rebalancing is monthly, with three orders at the month-end close. Weights change slowly (Figure 2), so annual turnover is only 33% of the portfolio, and costs reduced the return by just 0.04% a year (gross 6.61%, net 6.57%). With 50 bps per unit traded the Sharpe ratio would be 0.69, and the Sharpe advantage over the 60/40 only disappears at about 199 bps per unit traded, far above realistic costs for these ETFs. Quarterly rebalancing (Table 2) is a cheaper option for small accounts with little loss of performance.

Investing from Portugal. EU retail investors generally cannot buy US-domiciled ETFs (they have no PRIIPs key information document), so the portfolio would be implemented with UCITS equivalents: an S&P 500 UCITS ETF, a US Treasury 7–10 year UCITS ETF and a physically backed gold ETC (for example from the iShares, Vanguard, Invesco or Xtrackers ranges; product, cost and domicile to be checked in each key information document). The backtest is in US dollars: a euro investor also bears EUR/USD risk unless euro-hedged share classes are used, at an extra cost. Taxes are not modelled.

Main risks and limitations.

  • Concentration: on average 56% of the capital is in one bond fund. The portfolio is exposed to rising interest rates and inflation, as 2022 showed; it also holds only US assets plus gold.
  • Correlation risk: the rule ignores correlations. When stocks and bonds fall together, the diversification the client relies on weakens.
  • Volatility is backward-looking: weights react only after volatility rises, so a sudden crash is not avoided (e.g. March 2020).
  • Look-ahead bias: avoided by construction: the weights at month-end t use data up to t only and are applied from the next trading day; the notebook checks this by recomputing the weights with the data cut at t. Trading exactly at the close is slightly optimistic; the one-day-lag test in Table 2 shows the effect is negligible.
  • Survivorship and selection bias: the backtest uses three broad ETFs that still trade today; funds that closed are not in the choice set, and our choice of asset classes is made with today's knowledge. The largest such effect is gold, which rose strongly over the sample: the results would be weaker without it. Inside SPY, the index replacing failing companies is part of the investable return, not a bias of the backtest.
  • Sample dependence: 2006–2026 includes a long decline in interest rates, which helped bonds until 2020. Past performance does not guarantee future results.
